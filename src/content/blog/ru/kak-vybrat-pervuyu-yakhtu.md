Buying a yacht for the first time raises questions that few advisors answer clearly. How long should it be? How many cabins do you actually need? Is a flybridge worth the cost? And what does the builder's reputation tell you about what you'll experience five years in?

The honest answer is that the right yacht depends on fewer variables than you might think — and almost all of them are about how you intend to use the water, not about the specification sheet.

## Start with the water, not the specification

Before speaking to any builder, answer three questions clearly.

**Where will you be?** Coastal day cruising demands a different hull than open-sea passages. A 28-foot open deck is brilliant in the Mediterranean on a summer afternoon. It is less pleasant crossing the Bay of Biscay in October.

**Who will be aboard?** Solo sailing and family holidays call for different layouts. A weekender with two forward berths makes sense if you have young children. A saloon-focused flybridge makes more sense if you entertain guests overnight.

**How will you use it most weeks?** The yacht you own is not the yacht you imagined buying. Most owners spend 80% of their time on day trips. If that describes you, optimise for simplicity — fewer systems to maintain, quicker to rig, easier to handle alone.

## Length is a proxy, not a goal

A common mistake is to anchor the choice on length. Length is a proxy for volume, range, and — increasingly — for berth fees. It is not a measure of quality or even of comfort.

A well-designed 30-foot hull from a builder like Saxdor or Parker will outperform a poorly considered 40-footer in almost every real-world measure that matters: fuel economy, sea-keeping in a chop, and the confidence of a captain who can actually handle the boat singlehanded.

## The sea trial tells you what the brochure cannot

There is no substitute for time on the water. Ask to sea-trial the model in conditions that reflect your actual usage — not flat-calm harbour water, but in a light chop with realistic load aboard.

What to feel for: how the hull sits at anchor, whether the helm has any deadzone, how the layout works when someone is cooking and someone else is moving forward. These are the moments the specification sheet cannot describe.
